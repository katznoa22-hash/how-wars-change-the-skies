Aviation Network Fundamentals

The Invisible Highways of the Sky

A route connects airports through airspace. Geography gives a starting point; permissions, weather and air traffic services shape the flight.

Great-circle distance

On a spherical Earth, a great-circle arc is the shortest surface path between two points. It often looks curved on a flat map. Our model uses this as a theoretical baseline, not a recorded flight path.

Sovereign airspace

The Chicago Convention recognizes state sovereignty over territorial airspace (Article 1). Scheduled international services need the relevant state authorization (Article 6). Different rules apply to non-scheduled flights.

FIR: Flight Information Region

An FIR is an area where flight information and alerting services are provided. Its boundary need not match a country border. A restriction may cover only part of an FIR, selected altitudes or specific operators.

Verified legal referenceICAO · Chicago Convention, Articles 1, 5, 6 and 9

Operational Mechanics

What Happens When Airspace Closes?

Closures, regulator advice and an airline’s own risk assessment are different inputs. None can be replaced by choosing the cheapest route.

STEP 01

Identify the disruption

Conflict may create hazards or trigger restrictions on particular operators.

STEP 02

Read the notices

A NOTAM is an official notice of operational information. States publish restrictions; EASA also publishes conflict-zone advice and bulletins.

STEP 03

Review affected flights

Airlines and air traffic services assess whether departures, rerouting, holding or diversions are needed.

STEP 04

Replan the operation

A feasible alternative must account for permissions, aircraft performance, fuel, crew and airport availability.

Two Conflicts, Global Consequences

Two documented examples explain the mechanism. The route models on the following slides are separate teaching scenarios, not reconstructions of these events.

Airline-reported route changes

Finnair reports bypassing Russian airspace on services to Asia. This is an operator-specific example: access and restrictions differ between airlines and dates. It does not establish a single global average detour.

Why the geography matters

A northern European hub can lose access to short eastbound paths. Longer alternatives affect flying time, aircraft rotations and fuel requirements. Our LHR–HND and HEL–ICN examples quantify chosen hypothetical waypoint paths.

A short-lived regional disruption

EASA’s 14 April 2024 notice described temporary airspace closures in Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq and Iran during military exchanges on 13–14 April. The notice said the relevant NOTAMs had expired during 14 April.

Keep the time window visible

Temporary closures can disrupt connectivity even when a destination is elsewhere. The presentation does not claim those April 2024 closures remain active. Red country shading is geographic context, not an exact closure footprint.

Executive Management Strategy

Airlines Under Pressure: The Four Dilemmas

Safety and legal feasibility are prerequisites. Within those constraints, managers compare costs, schedule resilience and passenger needs.

1 · Operating expense

Longer flights consume more fuel and can increase crew and maintenance costs. The size of the effect depends on the operation; Slide 7 lets you vary selected assumptions.

2 · Aircraft rotations

Extra flight time can reduce time available for the next departure. Whether delays spread depends on schedule buffers, spare aircraft and connection timing.

3 · Network continuity

Suspension may reduce connectivity, while partner carriage can preserve some service. Airport slot implications depend on the jurisdiction, season and any applicable relief.

4 · Passenger trust

Clear communication, rebooking and refund arrangements matter during disruption. No cost comparison establishes that a flight is safe to operate.

Managerial interpretation

There is no universal winning strategy. The next slide provides a transparent teaching exercise rather than a forecast of airline performance.

Synthesis & Sources

The Future of Aviation Resilience

Restrictions change the usable network. Longer routes can raise operating demands, but the result depends on geography, the airline and the assumptions.

Plan flexible networks

Consider alternative routings, partners and recovery plans. Aircraft range alone does not resolve permissions, crew, airport or safety constraints.

Keep evidence visible

Separate dated historical events from current conditions and theoretical models. A country outline does not establish which airspace was closed.

Test assumptions

Distance is reproducible; costs depend on inputs. Use sensitivity analysis and avoid presenting invented scores as measured airline performance.
